How to End Gerrymandering in Texas

A fair, redrawn Texas map built on multiwinner districts and proportional representation.

← Back to all states

Texas has 29 million people, four major metropolitan areas, and an economy so large that if it were an independent country it would rank as the 8th largest economy in the world by GDP, larger than Brazil, Canada, or Russia. Its political diversity is masked due to its status as one of the most gerrymandered states under the current system: only 1 district out of Texas's 38 is competitive heading into 2024. Texas sends 25 Republicans and 13 Democrats to Congress, despite Democrats making up about 45% of voters statewide.

This multimember map ungerrymanders Texas and would seat representatives that span the gamut of Texas's political diversity. Because all twelve of Texas's districts have five seats, I project that all twelve districts would be competitive between parties. Electing rural Texas Democrats and urban Texas Republicans would go far to bridge the ideological divide.

In total, Texas could plausibly elect a Congressional delegation of 23 Republicans, 23 Democrats, 9 Libertarians, and 5 Progressives.

The Republican Party could be challenged by a Libertarian Party in 11 of the 12 districts, the only exception being the Democratic stronghold of TX-9 (Houston). But even there, a Libertarian candidate could try their luck and perhaps capture the mere 17% of the vote needed to win one seat. Libertarian candidates would likely safely win one seat and could compete for a second seat in TX-2 (Lubbock, north Texas), TX-3 (northeast Texas), TX-6 (Waco, Longview), and TX-7 (Beaumont, east Houston metro). In total, a Libertarian Party could compete for 15 of Texas's 60 seats, 25%, and turn Texas into a true three-party state.

From the left, the Democratic Party could be challenged by a Progressive Party in seven districts, mostly centered around Texas's major cities. Progressives could safely win one seat in TX-5 (Dallas) and safely win one and compete for a second seat in TX-9. In three districts, voters could plausibly elect one candidate from four different parties: TX-4 (Fort Worth), TX-8 (west and south Houston metro), and TX-12 (Corpus Christi, Reynosa).
